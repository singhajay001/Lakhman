# Australian Spirits Have Quietly Got Very Good

Fifteen years ago, "Australian spirits" mostly meant a handful of Tasmanian
whisky distilleries and a lot of goodwill. That is no longer the position. The
category has gone from curiosity to genuinely competitive, and in one or two
areas it is doing things that cannot be done anywhere else.

## Whisky: the climate does the work

The most interesting thing about Australian whisky is not a technique. It is
the weather.

Maturation is an exchange between spirit and oak, and it speeds up with heat.
Warm conditions push spirit deeper into the wood; cool conditions pull it back
out. The more the temperature swings, the more of this happens. Scotland's
climate is cool and stable, which is why a Scotch needs a decade before it
starts saying much.

Australian conditions are warmer and swing harder. The result is that whisky
here reaches maturity considerably faster. A four or five year old Australian
single malt can show a depth of oak character that would take a Speyside malt
twelve years to develop.

This cuts both ways. Faster is not automatically better, and it is possible to
over-oak a spirit here in a way that is hard to do in Scotland. The distilleries
doing this well are watching the cask rather than the calendar.

It also explains why you should stop using age statements to judge Australian
whisky. A number that means one thing in Dufftown means something quite
different in the Hunter Valley.

**Archie Rose Single Malt** is the Sydney example most people meet first —
distilled in Botany, and notable for using multiple malt types rather than one,
which is unusual anywhere. It does not taste like Scotch, and it is not trying
to.

## Gin: where the native botanicals actually matter

Gin is the category where Australia has the clearest natural advantage, because
the flora is genuinely unlike anywhere else and much of it is aromatic.

The traditional gin botanical set — juniper, coriander, angelica, orris, citrus
peel — was assembled in Europe from what grew in Europe. Australian distillers
have a second pantry available:

**Lemon myrtle** carries more citral than lemon peel does, and gives a lifted,
almost lemongrass-like citrus. **Tasmanian pepperberry** brings heat and a dark
berry note. **Finger lime** gives sharp, contained bursts of acidity.
**Wattleseed** is roasted and nutty, closer to coffee. **Strawberry gum** and
**riberry** both push toward red fruit.

Used badly, this becomes a novelty — a gin that tastes like a native ingredients
checklist. Used well, it produces something a London Dry structurally cannot:
citrus with a different shape, and a peppery finish that is not black pepper.

The test is whether the juniper is still in charge. If you cannot find it, what
you have is a flavoured spirit wearing a gin label.

## Agave: the genuinely new thing

This is the development worth paying attention to.

Agave grows in parts of Australia, and several distilleries are now making
agave spirit here. It cannot legally be called tequila — that designation is
protected and requires production in specific regions of Mexico, in the same
way Champagne is protected. So it is sold as agave spirit.

What makes it interesting is not that it imitates tequila. It is that agave
grown in different soil, in a different climate, roasted and fermented by people
without a two-hundred-year house style to defend, produces something with its
own character. Some of it leans closer to mezcal's roasted register; some is
cleaner and more like a blanco.

**Karu Distillery** in Richmond, New South Wales is one of the producers doing
this — their rested agave spirit sits in the same conversation as a reposado
without pretending to be one.

It is early. The category is small, the plants take years to mature, and not
everything being made is good. But it is the first time an entirely new
agave-producing region has emerged in a long while, and being near it is a
reason to pay attention now rather than in ten years.

## What to actually buy

Some honest guidance on where the value sits.

**If you drink Scotch**, an Australian single malt is a real change rather than
a variation. Expect more oak influence at a younger age, and expect to pay more
than the equivalent Scotch — small-scale production and Australian excise both
push the price up.

**If you drink gin**, this is the easiest entry point and the best value in
the category. The quality floor is high and the price gap to imported gin is
small.

**If you drink tequila or mezcal**, Australian agave spirit is the most
interesting thing on this list and the one most likely to surprise you.

## On the price question

Australian spirits are not cheap, and the reason is not margin. Excise on
spirits in Australia is among the highest in the world and is indexed twice a
year. A significant share of the shelf price of any Australian-made spirit is
tax, before the distillery has covered a single cost.

That is worth knowing when comparing an Australian gin to an imported one and
wondering why the local product costs more. It is not the distillery.
