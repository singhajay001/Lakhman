# Stocking a Bar for an Event

Most people planning drinks for an event get one of two things wrong: they
buy far too much of the wrong thing, or they run out of ice at 8pm. Both are
avoidable with an hour of arithmetic.

This is how we work it out when someone calls us about a birthday, an
engagement or a company function. It is a planning method rather than a
shopping list, because the right list depends on who is coming.

## Start with the guests, not the bottles

Before any quantity, answer three questions.

**How long is the event?** A three-hour function and a six-hour one are
completely different problems. Consumption is not linear either — it is highest
in the first ninety minutes, then drops.

**Is there food, and when?** Substantial food slows everything down. Canapés
for three hours does not.

**Who is actually coming?** A room of thirty people in their sixties drinks
differently from thirty in their twenties, and a work function drinks
differently from a wedding. If a meaningful number of guests are drivers,
pregnant, observant, in recovery or simply not drinking, that changes the ratio
more than anything else on this page.

## The arithmetic

A widely used planning baseline is **one drink per guest for the first hour,
then one every hour after**, with the understanding that actual consumption
varies enormously and that your job is to have enough, not to have it all
consumed.

For 50 guests over four hours, that is roughly 250 drinks across the whole
room — but only if every guest drinks throughout, which never happens. In
practice, planning for **60 to 70% of that figure** and keeping a small reserve
is closer to what actually gets poured.

### What a bottle gives you

| Format | Standard pour | Drinks per bottle |
|---|---|---|
| Spirits, 700ml | 30ml | ~23 |
| Spirits, 700ml | 45ml (realistic) | ~15 |
| Wine, 750ml | 150ml | 5 |
| Sparkling, 750ml | 120ml flute | 6 |

**Plan on the 45ml line for spirits.** Unless a professional is pouring,
nobody measures, and the difference between the two rows is the difference
between having enough and a panicked trip to the bottle shop.

### A worked example: 50 guests, four hours, food served

- Assume 35 of the 50 are drinking with any regularity
- Assume around 3 drinks each over four hours → **~105 drinks**
- Split: 45% wine and sparkling, 35% spirits, 20% beer or non-alcoholic
- **Wine/sparkling:** ~47 drinks → 9 bottles, round to 12
- **Spirits:** ~37 drinks → at 15 per bottle, 3 bottles, round to 4
- **Beer/NA:** ~21 servings, plus generous non-alcoholic beyond that

Four bottles of spirits for fifty people surprises everyone. It is right. The
common error is buying a bottle of everything — eight or nine spirits for an
event that will pour three.

## Buy narrow, not wide

The most useful advice we give is to stock **two or three drinks properly**
rather than everything badly.

Pick one clear spirit and one dark, or build around two signature cocktails and
one good wine. A well-made gin and tonic that does not run out beats a bar with
eleven bottles where five are untouched and the popular one is gone by nine.

If you are running cocktails, pick ones that batch. A margarita, a negroni or a
spritz can be pre-mixed in quantity and poured over ice. A drink that must be
shaken individually will bottleneck the moment more than ten people want one.

## The things people forget

**Ice.** Roughly **1kg per guest**, more in a Sydney summer. Ice is used for
chilling bottles as well as filling glasses, and it is the one item you cannot
improvise at 8pm. Buy more than feels sensible.

**Mixers.** Budget around three mixers per spirit drink. Tonic, soda, ginger
beer, cola — and buy small bottles or cans rather than large, because a
two-litre bottle goes flat long before it is finished.

**Citrus.** One lime yields about 30ml of juice and eight wedges. Count
backwards from your drinks list.

**Glassware.** Plan 1.5 to 2 glasses per guest — people put them down and lose
them. Hire is usually cheaper than replacing your own.

**Somewhere to put rubbish**, and a plan for empties. Unglamorous, always
forgotten.

## The non-alcoholic half

This is the part most guides treat as an afterthought, and it is the part most
likely to be noticed.

Plan for **at least a third** of your guests to be drinking little or nothing —
designated drivers, people who are pregnant, people on medication, people who
simply do not drink. That proportion is higher than most hosts assume and it is
rising.

The thing that matters is not quantity but quality. Soft drink and tap water is
provision; a proper non-alcoholic option is hospitality. Good tonic with
grapefruit and ice in a real glass, a decent ginger beer, a non-alcoholic
sparkling — any of these mean a guest who is not drinking is not standing there
holding a plastic cup of lemonade feeling like a problem to be managed.

Make sure water is visible and free-standing, not something people have to ask
for.

## Serving responsibly

If you are hosting, you carry some responsibility for how people leave.

- **Never top up a glass mid-drink.** It makes it impossible for anyone to
  track what they have had, including them.
- **Serve food throughout**, not just at the start.
- **Do not serve anyone under 18.** In NSW it is an offence to supply alcohol
  to a minor on private premises without their parent's or guardian's
  authority, and rules differ by state — check the ones that apply where your
  event is.
- **Do not serve anyone who is intoxicated**, and have a plan for getting them
  home. Rideshare details on the invitation cost nothing.
- **If you are hiring staff**, use someone with a current RSA.
