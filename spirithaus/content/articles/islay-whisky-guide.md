# Islay Whisky: Why It Tastes Like Smoke, Salt and Iodine

Islay is a small island off Scotland's west coast — about 25 kilometres across,
with a population smaller than a Sydney suburb. It produces a whisky style so
distinctive that people who love it and people who cannot stand it are usually
describing the same glass accurately.

If you have ever been handed an Islay malt and thought *this tastes like a
hospital, or a bonfire, or the inside of a smokehouse* — you were not wrong.
That is the point. Here is where it comes from.

## Peat is the whole story

Whisky starts with barley, and barley has to be dried before it can be malted.
Islay has almost no trees. What it has, in enormous quantity, is peat — layers
of compressed, half-decomposed bog vegetation built up over thousands of years,
cut into bricks and burned as fuel.

When distillers on Islay dried their barley over peat fires, the smoke did
something more than heat the grain. Peat smoke is full of **phenols** — aromatic
compounds that settle onto the husk of the barley and stay there. They survive
malting. They survive fermentation. They survive distillation. By the time the
spirit reaches the cask, the smoke is not a flavour that has been added to the
whisky. It is part of its structure.

Distillers measure this in parts per million of phenol. A Speyside malt might
sit near zero. An Islay malt can run from the mid-twenties into the fifties and
beyond. That number is measured in the malted barley rather than the finished
whisky, so it is a rough guide rather than a scale of intensity — but it tells
you what kind of dram you are holding.

## Where the salt comes from

The other half of the Islay signature is maritime: brine, seaweed, wet rope,
something like iodine. Distilleries on the island sit close to the water, and
casks have historically matured in warehouses where sea air moves through the
building for a decade or more.

How much of that genuinely transfers into the spirit is argued about, and there
is more romance in the standard explanation than chemistry strictly supports.
What is not in dispute is the result: Islay whisky tastes coastal in a way that
inland Scotch does not.

## The three you are most likely to meet

Islay has a handful of working distilleries, and three turn up more than the
rest on Australian shelves.

**Laphroaig 10 Year Old** is the most uncompromising of the common three.
Distilled at Port Ellen on the south coast, with barley dried over peat cut
from the distillery's own beds. The classic descriptor is *medicinal* — iodine,
antiseptic, tar — and people mean it as praise. If you want to know
immediately whether peat is for you, this answers the question in one sip.

**Lagavulin 16 Year Old** sits between Laphroaig and Ardbeg on the same stretch
of shore, and sixteen years is a long maturation by Islay standards. The extra
time in cask rounds the smoke off. It is still unmistakably peated, but there
is sweetness underneath — dried fruit, a little sherry influence — that the
younger bottlings do not have. This is the one most often described as the
benchmark.

**Caol Ila** is the gentler entry. Distilled on the Sound of Islay on the
island's east side, it carries the smoke more lightly than its southern
neighbours, with a cleaner, more citrus-inflected character. If Laphroaig is a
test, Caol Ila is an introduction.

## How to drink it without wasting it

A few things worth knowing before you open one.

**Add water.** Not ice — water, a few drops at a time. Peated whisky at cask
strength is dense, and a little water opens it up rather than diluting it. Add,
taste, add again. You can always add more; you cannot take it out.

**Give it air.** Pour it and leave it five minutes. Heavily peated spirit
changes noticeably in the glass as the most volatile compounds lift off.

**Do not start with the biggest one.** If you are introducing someone to Islay,
open the Caol Ila, not the Laphroaig. The usual reason people decide they hate
peat is that their first encounter was with the most extreme example available.

**Eat something with it.** Peated whisky and oily fish, smoked meat, or a hard
washed-rind cheese work in a way that is not subtle. Blue cheese in particular
is a well-worn pairing for a reason.

## Is it worth the jump?

Honest answer: Islay whisky is the most polarising category we stock, and no
amount of description settles it. Some people find the smoke opens up a whole
corner of whisky they did not know existed. Others find it tastes like a
chimney and never come back.

The reason it is worth finding out is that nothing else tastes like it. Peat is
not a variation on Speyside — it is a different proposition entirely, and if it
lands for you, it tends to land permanently.
