# Japanese Whisky and Japanese Gin

Japan makes some of the most sought-after whisky in the world, and one of the
most interesting gins. Neither category is doing anything Scotland or England
invented — both are doing it with a different set of priorities.

## Japanese whisky started as a copy and stopped being one

The method came from Scotland. Masataka Taketsuru studied chemistry in Glasgow
in the 1920s and took Scotch production back with him. Pot stills, malted
barley, the lot.

What changed is a structural quirk. In Scotland, distilleries trade casks
constantly — a blender can source malt from dozens of houses. Japanese
distilleries historically did not do this with each other. If you wanted variety
for your blends, you had to create it yourself, inside your own walls.

So they did. Different still shapes, different yeast strains, different
fermentation times, different cask types, all under one roof. A single Japanese
distillery might produce dozens of distinct spirit styles where a Scottish one
produces a handful.

That is why Japanese blends have their reputation. The blender is working with
a deliberately constructed palette rather than an assembled one.

### The labelling problem, and what fixed it

For years, "Japanese whisky" was not a protected term. Spirit could be distilled
elsewhere, shipped in, bottled in Japan, and sold as Japanese. Plenty was.

In 2021 the Japan Spirits & Liqueurs Makers Association introduced standards
requiring that mashing, fermentation, distillation, maturation of at least three
years and bottling all take place in Japan. It is an industry standard rather
than a law, so it is worth knowing that the label on an older or cheaper bottle
may not mean what it appears to.

The established names — Suntory's Yamazaki, Hakushu and Chita, and Nikka's
distilleries — were never the problem.

### Two worth knowing

**Hibiki Japanese Harmony** is Suntory's flagship blend: malt from Yamazaki and
Hakushu with grain from Chita, no age statement. The character is exactly what
the name claims — nothing sticks out, everything sits together. Honey, orchard
fruit, a faint incense note from mizunara oak in part of the blend.

**Hakushu 12 Year Old** comes from the forest distillery in the Southern
Japanese Alps, and is the green one: herbaceous, faintly smoky, with a
freshness that people describe in terms of mint and cucumber more often than
fruit. It is unlike anything in Scotland.

### Mizunara

Worth a paragraph of its own. Mizunara is a Japanese oak that is, by any
practical measure, a bad cask wood. It is porous, prone to leaking, hard to
work, and needs to be very old before it yields usable staves.

It is used anyway, because it contributes something no other oak does —
sandalwood, incense, a coconut-like sweetness. Usually it appears as a component
of a blend rather than a full maturation, because a whisky matured entirely in
mizunara can tip into perfume.

## Japanese gin: the same instinct, applied to botanicals

Gin has one legal requirement: juniper must be the dominant flavour. Everything
else is open. London Dry — the dominant style — layers juniper with coriander,
angelica, citrus peel and orris, and leans dry and piney.

Japanese gin takes the same frame and swaps in local botanicals.

**Roku** is the clearest example. The name means "six", for six Japanese
botanicals used alongside the traditional eight: sakura flower and sakura leaf,
sencha and gyokuro tea, sanshō pepper and yuzu peel. Each is harvested in its
own season and distilled separately before blending, because they do not all
give up their character under the same conditions.

The result reads as gin — the juniper is there — but the citrus is yuzu rather
than lemon, there is a green tea note through the middle, and sanshō pepper
leaves a faint tingle on the finish that is closer to Sichuan pepper than to
black.

**How to use it:** a gin and tonic with grapefruit rather than lime lets the
yuzu sit forward. In a Negroni it holds up better than most contemporary gins
because the pepper has somewhere to go against the Campari. In a martini it is
a matter of taste — some find the tea note gets lost under vermouth.

## Why any of this matters

The shared thread is that both categories are the product of constraint. Japanese
distillers could not buy variety, so they built it. Japanese gin makers were
working in a country with a deep tea and citrus culture and no juniper tradition,
so they used what was to hand.

That tends to produce more interesting spirits than abundance does.
