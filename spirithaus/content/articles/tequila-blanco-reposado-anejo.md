# Blanco, Reposado, Añejo

Tequila spent a long time in Australia being the thing you did shots of at
21st birthdays, and it is still recovering. The category deserves better,
because at the top end it is as considered a spirit as single malt — and the
labelling, once you know the three words, is more honest than most.

## The one thing to check first

Before blanco, reposado or añejo, look for **100% agave**.

Tequila must be made from blue agave grown in designated regions of Mexico,
mostly Jalisco. But the rules allow two grades. If the bottle says *100%
agave*, every fermentable sugar came from agave. If it does not say that, it is
a **mixto** — legally as little as 51% agave, with the rest made up from other
sugars, usually cane.

Mixto is where tequila's reputation for rough mornings comes from. It is also
most of what gets poured as shots. If you buy nothing else from this article,
buy the habit of checking for those two words.

## The three ages

Everything else on the label comes down to how long the spirit sat in oak.

**Blanco** (also *silver* or *plata*) is bottled straight after distillation,
or rested under two months. Nothing between you and the agave: it tastes green,
peppery, citrus-bright, sometimes vegetal or faintly saline. This is tequila
with its actual character exposed, which is why the best distilleries are often
judged on their blanco.

**Reposado** — "rested" — spends two to twelve months in oak. The agave is
still the lead, but oak rounds the edges and adds vanilla, light caramel and
warmth. Reposado is the diplomat of the category: good in cocktails that need a
bit more body, good to sip, not committed to either.

**Añejo** — "aged" — spends one to three years in oak, in barrels no larger
than 600 litres. By this point the oak is doing a lot of the talking: caramel,
dried fruit, baking spice, a colour closer to whisky. Sipping territory.

**Extra añejo** goes beyond three years and sits in the same conversation, and
often the same price bracket, as aged Scotch.

There is no ladder here. An excellent blanco beats a mediocre añejo, and paying
for age you are then going to drown in lime juice is money thrown away.

## Which one for what

**Margaritas: blanco.** The drink is agave, lime and orange liqueur in balance.
Oak muddies it. This is the single most common mistake in home bartending — the
instinct that the more expensive aged bottle must make the better cocktail.
It makes a more expensive, worse cocktail.

**Palomas and long drinks: blanco or reposado.** Grapefruit and soda have
enough going on that either works.

**Old Fashioned variations and sipping: reposado or añejo.** Here the oak is the
point.

**Shots: none of the above, ideally.** If tequila is going to be drunk fast and
cold, the good bottle is wasted. This is not snobbery so much as arithmetic.

**Don Julio Blanco** is the reference point we most often reach for — clean,
properly agave-forward, and it behaves exactly as a blanco should in a
margarita.

## Tequila and mezcal

Briefly, because it comes up constantly.

Tequila is a type of mezcal, in the same way that Cognac is a type of brandy.
Tequila must be blue agave, from specific regions, and the agave is usually
steamed in ovens or autoclaves. Mezcal can be made from dozens of agave
species, across a wider area, and is traditionally roasted in earthen pits over
hot rocks.

That pit roasting is where mezcal's smoke comes from. It is not peat, and it is
not a flaw — it is the method. If you have tried mezcal and found it smoky to
the point of distraction, the useful comparison is Islay whisky: an intense
style that some palates take to immediately and others never do.

## A note on the agave itself

Blue agave takes somewhere between six and eight years to mature before it can
be harvested, and it is harvested once. The plant does not regrow. Every bottle
represents the better part of a decade of growing, and the jimadores who cut
and trim the piñas by hand are doing skilled work with a sharpened blade.

That is worth knowing mostly because it reframes the price. A cheap tequila is
cheap because something was skipped — usually the agave percentage, sometimes
the maturation time of the plant itself.
