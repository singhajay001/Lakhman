# Choosing Your First Single Malt

Whisky has a vocabulary problem. Walk into it cold and you meet single malt,
blended, cask strength, non-chill-filtered, Speyside, sherry finish and a
number on the bottle that may or may not mean what you think — all before
anyone has poured anything.

Most of it is simpler than it sounds. Here is what actually matters when you
are buying your first bottle.

## Single malt vs blended: not a quality ladder

This is the one most often misunderstood.

**Single malt** means the whisky is made from malted barley, at one distillery.
"Single" refers to the single distillery — not a single cask, and not a single
batch. A 12 year old single malt is usually a marriage of many casks from that
one distillery.

**Blended Scotch** combines malt whisky from several distilleries with grain
whisky, which is made from other cereals in a continuous still and is lighter
in character. Blending is a craft in its own right, and the blender's job is
consistency: making this year's bottle taste like last year's.

Blended is not the budget version of single malt. It is a different product
with a different goal. Chivas Regal 18 and Johnnie Walker 18 are both blends,
and both sit well above most entry single malts in price. What is true is that
single malts tend to have more pronounced regional and distillery character,
which is usually what people are looking for when they start exploring.

## What the number means

An age statement tells you the age of the **youngest** whisky in the bottle.
A 12 year old may well contain older whisky, but nothing younger than twelve
years.

Two things follow from that.

First, older is not automatically better. Time in oak adds vanilla, spice and
tannin, and eventually it starts to bury whatever the distillery's spirit
actually tastes like. There is a point where you are mostly tasting the cask.

Second, no age statement does not mean bad. Hibiki Japanese Harmony carries no
age statement and is one of the most admired blends in the world. Aberlour
A'bunadh has none either, and is sought after precisely because each batch is
bottled at cask strength without one.

## The regions, briefly

Scotland's whisky regions are a rough guide rather than a rulebook, but they
help.

**Speyside** is where most of Scotland's distilleries are, in a small area
around the River Spey. The house style is fruit and sweetness — apple, pear,
honey, often with sherry cask influence. This is the safest place to start.

**Islay** is smoke. Peated, coastal, polarising. Wonderful, but not a first
bottle. We have written about it separately in
[the Islay guide](/blogs/news/islay-whisky-guide).

**Highland** is the largest region and the least consistent as a style, running
from light and floral to rich and heavy.

**Lowland** tends to be lighter and grassier. **Campbeltown** is tiny and
tends toward oily and slightly briny.

## Four honest starting points

**Glenfiddich 12 Year Old** is the benchmark, and it is the benchmark for a
reason. Speyside, Dufftown, matured in a mix of American and European oak.
Pear, a little oak, nothing challenging. If you want to know what "standard
Speyside" tastes like so you can measure everything else against it, this is
the reference sample.

**Aberlour 12 Year Old Double Cask Matured** is the sherried option. Matured in
both traditional oak and sherry casks, which gives it more dried fruit and
Christmas-cake weight than the Glenfiddich. If you already know you like richer,
sweeter flavours, start here instead.

**BenRiach The Original Ten** is a good third way — Speyside again, but
matured across three cask types, so it sits somewhere between the two above
without committing hard to either.

**Chivas Regal 12** is worth naming because a good blend is an entirely
legitimate place to begin, and it costs meaningfully less than the malts above.
If the goal is to find out whether you enjoy Scotch at all before spending
three figures, this is the sensible experiment.

## How to drink it

Nobody is grading you, but a few things genuinely help.

**Use a glass that narrows at the top.** A tumbler lets the aroma escape. A
copita, a Glencairn, or even a small wine glass concentrates it, and most of
what you taste is actually smell.

**Add a few drops of water.** This is not heresy — it is what distillery
managers do. Water lowers the alcohol enough to let other aromas lift.

**Ice is a trade-off, not a crime.** It mutes aroma and flavour as it dilutes.
If you prefer it cold, a single large cube melts more slowly than several small
ones.

**Take your time.** Whisky at 40% and up numbs the palate quickly. The second
sip usually tells you more than the first.
