Trafalgar Grocery & Liquor · Channel review

Why Uber Eats orders halved

Seven months of order data says the store is not losing customers to a worse offer — there are fewer customers reaching it. Order volume is down 43% since mid-May while average basket value is up 17%. The store sits beside Macquarie University, and the monthly shape follows the academic year closely enough that seasonality explains most of the fall — but not all of it. One test settles the rest.

The shape of the problem

Volume fell off a cliff after April, and never fully came back

Orders per day held between 4.1 and 4.6 from February to April, then dropped to roughly 2.1 through June and July. August recovered to 3.16 — better, but still a third below the April run rate.

Rising basket value alongside falling orders is the diagnostic

If the offer, the pricing or the range had got worse, baskets would shrink too. They grew. What is missing is the marginal shopper — the one who was browsing, saw the store, and bought two things. The committed customers are still ordering, and ordering more. That is an impressions problem, not a product problem.

Ruling out the alternatives

Every day and every hour fell by roughly the same amount

This is the test that matters. If trading hours had been cut, specific hours would vanish. If a liquor competitor had opened, the late-night peak would have taken the damage. If it were seasonal, weekends would hold up better than weekdays. None of that happened — the whole curve moved down together.

Two further checks point the same way. Trading hours were unchanged all year — the first order of the day lands at 10am and the last before midnight, in every single month. And the store was almost never dark: across 222 days there were only three two-day gaps with no orders, and 23 zero-order days in total, which at two to four orders a day is ordinary rather than alarming.

What does change is the calendar — but not the retail one. The store is at Shop 5, 1 Trafalgar Place, Marsfield NSW 2112, next door to Macquarie University. Read the months against the academic year and they line up:

A student catchment emptying out over the mid-year break would produce exactly what the data shows: every weekday and every daypart falling together, late-night share holding, and a recovery when semester resumes. It fits better than the explanation I reached for before seeing the address — an expiring new-merchant boost would decay and stay decayed, and would not bounce back in August.

But seasonality does not explain all of it

August ran at 3.16 orders per day against April's 4.60. Both are mid-semester months, so they should be comparable — and August is still 31% down. Something beyond the calendar is taking a share of that gap, and September so far is running weaker still.

One check settles it, and you already have the data. Pull your in-store sales for the same months. If in-store dipped through June and July too, the catchment emptied and Uber Eats behaved normally. If in-store held steady while Uber Eats halved, the problem is on the platform and the visibility work below is urgent.

Which one it is changes what you can measure

Either MyFoodLink is not writing Uber-side promotions back into the order export — in which case you are running offers and ads you have no way to evaluate, and cannot tell a profitable promotion from an expensive one — or these offers are recent and postdate the data, in which case the seven months of decline genuinely did run unpromoted.

Check the start dates in Merchant Manager. If it is the first case, that is a bigger problem than any single offer: you are spending on placement blind.

Who is actually buying

On Uber Eats, this is a bottle shop that also sells snacks

Not a supermarket. The data is unambiguous, and the merchandising should follow it.

The GST split says the same thing from the other direction. Alcohol and packaged goods carry GST; basic food does not — so the tax on each order is a reliable proxy for what was in the bag.

Four in five dollars are liquor, mixers, snacks and packaged goods. More than half of all orders contain no fresh food whatsoever. The peak is Saturday night at 9pm. Nobody is doing their weekly shop here — they are topping up a night in.

Merchandise for the actual occasion

The front of the Uber Eats store, the collections, the photography and any promotion should be built around Friday and Saturday evening top-ups: beer, wine and spirits with their mixers, ice, snacks and confectionery in the same collection. Presenting a general grocery aisle to a customer who is buying a Saturday night is a wasted first screen.

There is no beer, wine or spirits aisle anywhere in that list

Four in five of your dollars are alcohol and packaged goods. The word Cellars is in the store's own name. And a customer browsing the aisles cannot reach any of it — liquor is findable only by typing into search, or through the generic Spirits, Wine & Beer tiles Uber generates on the search screen, which only appear once the customer has already decided to search for it.

Electronics & Office has an aisle. Baby & Toddler has an aisle. Pets has an aisle. The category carrying 80% of your revenue does not.

To be precise about where the problem is not: at marketplace level the store is tagged Grocery · Convenience · Alcohol, so a customer searching Uber Eats for a bottle shop can find you. The breakage is inside the store — once they arrive, there is no aisle to walk down. This is the cheapest fix available and it is entirely in your back office. It will not explain the mid-year decline on its own, but it is a permanent brake on every browsing customer who arrives without a specific product in mind.

Check

The header shows the banner brand, not the store

The store record itself is correct — Trafalgar Supermarket And Cellars, tagged Grocery · Convenience · Alcohol, so alcohol intent is recognised at the marketplace level. But the storefront header displays Friendly Grocer, the banner brand shared with every other store in the group. Worth confirming which of the two a customer sees in search results and the home feed, since only one of them says Trafalgar.

Check

$7.99 delivery fee at zero kilometres

That is the fee shown standing at the store itself, so it is the floor rather than a distance-adjusted figure — plus service and small-order fees on top. On a $25 basket the customer is paying something close to half again in fees. Worth asking your account manager what is driving it and whether the $0 delivery fee offers can be extended, because it suppresses exactly the small top-up order this store is built around.

Check

Late-night liquor sits close to a regulatory boundary

Licence LIQP700301260 is a NSW packaged liquor licence, and NSW restricts the hours in which alcohol can be delivered. The 11pm hour is your second-best basket at $41.69, which puts your most valuable trading hour near whatever the cut-off is. Confirm the current rule against your licence conditions before extending hours or promoting into that window — the upside is real, and so is the exposure.

Working well

Item naming and photography are in good shape

Products read as Sheep Dog Whiskey, Peanut Butter (50mL) with 50 ml · 35% ABV beneath, and SunRice Thin Rice Cakes, Sour Cream & Chives at 160 g — brand, product, variant, size, with the unit metadata populated and real photography throughout. This is the part I expected to find broken and it is not. No work needed here.

Order economics

One in five orders is almost certainly losing money

Baskets are small: the median order is three items, and 42% of all orders contain exactly two. At the bottom of the range, the arithmetic stops working.

The sub-$10 tail

149 orders under $10, averaging $6.18. At an assumed 30% commission the store keeps about $4.32 — before the cost of the goods, before the bag, and before five to ten minutes of someone's time picking and packing it. These orders are 20% of volume and 3.7% of revenue.

They are not worthless — order frequency feeds ranking, which is the very thing that has slipped, so cutting them off with a high minimum basket would work against the recovery. And a small-order fee already appears in the customer's fee line, which means that lever is Uber's rather than yours. The realistic fix is basket size: occasion collections and bundles that turn a two-item order into a four-item one, which is exactly what a proper liquor aisle would support.

What to do, in order

The plan

The first two are diagnosis and should happen before anything else — they decide whether this is a catchment story or a platform one. The rest target visibility and discovery, because that is where the $29,000 sits — basket and margin work on 2.4 orders a day cannot match it. Items 2 and 4 cost nothing but an afternoon in the back office.

1

Compare in-store sales, month by month, against the Uber Eats line

Decides the rest

You already hold this data. If in-store trade dipped through June and July alongside Uber Eats, the university catchment emptied and the channel behaved normally — the job is then to plan around the academic year. If in-store held steady while Uber Eats halved, this is a platform visibility problem and everything below becomes urgent. Nothing else is worth deciding until you know which.

2

Pull impressions and menu views by month in Merchant Manager

This week

The other half of the same question. Impressions holding while orders fell points at conversion or the catchment; impressions falling points at ranking. Rating is already ruled out at 4.6, so check the missing-item rate while you are in there.

3

Create a Beer, Wine & Spirits aisle

Free · today

Your highest-revenue category currently has no entry point in the browse list. Add it, and put it near the top alongside Deals and Drinks. Then build the occasion collections beneath it — spirits with their mixers, beer with ice and snacks — so a Saturday-night shopper lands on a basket rather than an aisle.

4

Settle whether your promotions are being recorded

Blocks everything else

Offers and a sponsored item are live on the storefront but absent from every order in the export. Until that is resolved you cannot tell whether a promotion paid for itself, which makes every subsequent decision about spend a guess. Get the start dates from Merchant Manager, then ask MyFoodLink whether Uber-side promotions write back at all.

5

Fix the store name, and question the $7.99 delivery fee

Discovery

Make sure the name customers see carries "Trafalgar" so local search finds you, and ideally signals liquor. Separately, ask your account manager what sets the $7.99 floor fee and whether the $0 delivery offers can run more broadly — that fee lands hardest on the small top-up order that is most of your volume.

6

Confirm the uplift arithmetic covers the commission

Margin

Commission is charged on the marked-up price, so a 30% commission needs about a 43% uplift to hold the in-store dollar margin, not 30%. Check the current figure and tier it — hold near parity on the items people price-compare, take more on impulse and mixers. Note that a small-order fee already appears in the customer's fee line, so that lever is Uber's rather than yours; the sub-$10 tail has to be fixed through basket size, not a surcharge.

7

Fix the substitution and not-supplied tracking

Diagnostic

Ask MyFoodLink why both fields are empty on Uber orders. Without them you cannot see the missing-item rate, which is both a ranking factor and the most likely hidden cause of a rating slide.

8

Run the trading year on the academic calendar

Structural

If step 1 confirms a student catchment, stop treating the year as flat. Spend on offers and ads during session and especially in the first fortnight back, when habits form; pull spend right back over the mid-year and summer breaks rather than paying to reach an empty suburb. Build the range around what that customer actually buys late on a weekend.

9

Give the direct shop a reason to exist

Long game

An insert in every Uber Eats bag offering a discount for ordering direct next time costs almost nothing and converts marketplace customers into ones you keep the full margin on. Slow, compounding, and the only lever here that reduces the commission bill.

What this analysis could not see

Limits, and what would sharpen it

Both exports are order-level. There is no line-item or product detail in them, and no in-store sales, so nothing here is based on which products sold — the product mix above is inferred from GST rather than observed directly. The storefront findings come from screenshots of the live store taken on 7 September, not from a crawl, so they cover what a customer sees on the first few screens rather than the full catalogue.

  • A catalogue export — SKU, name, department, size, in-store and Uber price, image flag — would allow the naming-for-search audit and a real photo-gap list.
  • Line-item order detail would replace the GST proxy with actual product mix, and identify which items to bundle.
  • In-store sales for the same period would show which lines over-index on Uber Eats and which strong sellers are invisible there.
  • Cost prices would turn the uplift discussion into an actual margin model per department.
  • The Uber Eats commission rate from the merchant agreement — 30% is assumed throughout, and every dollar figure involving margin moves with it.
